When a debt collector contacts you, your first instinct might be to pay, argue, or ignore it. All three can create problems. A careful first move is to ask them, in writing, to document the debt they say you owe.

What is a debt validation letter?

A debt validation letter is a formal written request sent to a debt collector asking them to provide evidence of three things:

  • That you actually owe the debt
  • That the amount they claim is correct
  • That they have the legal authority to collect it

Under the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA), 15 U.S.C. § 1692g, a collector generally must respond to a timely written validation request. If they cannot validate the debt, they generally must stop collecting it. This page explains the process; it does not promise that any particular account will be changed or dropped from a credit report.

This site also has a shorter free template at businessLetter.html and a downloadable PDF on the Tools page. This page is the longer how-to.

Your rights under the FDCPA

Section 809 of the FDCPA describes your validation rights:

  • Within 5 days of first contacting you, a collector generally must send you a written notice with the amount of the debt and the name of the creditor
  • You have 30 days from receiving that notice to request validation in writing
  • Once you request validation in that window, the collector generally must pause collection of the disputed debt until they provide verification
  • Continuing to collect the disputed debt without providing that verification can be a violation of federal law

Why this matters: debts get sold and resold. Records get lost. Amounts get inflated. The original signed agreement — the actual proof that you agreed to pay a specific amount — often does not follow the debt through multiple sales. That is a reason the law requires collectors to document what they claim.

For a section-by-section overview, see the FDCPA summary.

When to send it (timing matters)

The 30-day window: If you send your validation request within 30 days of the collector's first written notice, they generally must stop collection activity on that debt until they respond. After 30 days, you can still request validation, but they may continue collection efforts while preparing a response. Send it as soon as you can if you are going to send one.

People often send a debt validation letter when:

  • A collector contacts you about a debt you don't recognize
  • The amount seems wrong or inflated
  • You believe the debt may be past the statute of limitations
  • The debt has been sold to a new collector
  • You see a collection on your credit report for the first time
  • You want documentation before paying anything

Free debt validation letter template

Copy and customize this letter. Send it by certified mail with return receipt requested, and keep copies.

[Your Full Name] [Your Address] [City, State ZIP] [Date] [Collection Agency Name] [Agency Address] [City, State ZIP] Re: Account # [Account Number from their notice] To Whom It May Concern: I am writing in response to your [letter/phone call] dated [date] regarding the above-referenced account. I am exercising my right under Section 809(b) of the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (15 U.S.C. § 1692g) to request validation of this debt. Please provide the following: 1. The amount of the debt and an itemized accounting of all fees, interest, and charges showing how the current balance was calculated 2. The name and address of the original creditor 3. A copy of the original signed agreement or contract between myself and the original creditor 4. Proof that your company has the legal authority to collect this debt (assignment or purchase agreement) 5. Proof that the statute of limitations has not expired on this debt 6. A copy of the last billing statement sent to me by the original creditor Until this debt is validated, I request that you cease all collection activity, including but not limited to phone calls, letters, and reporting to credit bureaus, as required by the FDCPA. Be advised that I am keeping records of all communications regarding this matter. Any continued collection activity prior to validation may constitute a violation of the FDCPA. Sincerely, [Your Signature] [Your Printed Name]

What happens after you send it

If they send documentation:

Review what they send carefully. Many collectors respond with a printout of their own records rather than original documentation from the creditor. A computer printout from the collector's system is not the same as a signed contract or original creditor statement. If their response does not include original documentation, you can send a follow-up letter pointing out what is missing.

If they do not respond within 30 days:

You can send a follow-up letter noting that they have not validated the debt. You may also dispute the account with the credit bureaus that are reporting it, and you may file a complaint with the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and your state attorney general. None of those steps guarantees a particular change to your report.

If they continue collecting without validating:

That can be a violation of federal law. Document everything. The FDCPA allows a consumer to sue for actual damages, statutory damages of up to $1,000, and attorney's fees in a successful case. That is a description of the law, not a prediction of your case. Consider talking with a consumer attorney about your facts.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Don't acknowledge the debt is yours. Avoid wording such as “I owe this but….” Simply request validation.
  • Don't rely on a phone call alone. Phone calls leave a weak paper trail. Communicate in writing and keep copies.
  • Don't send your only originals. Keep your originals. Send copies of any supporting documents.
  • Be cautious with a collector's online portal. Some systems include terms that can affect how a dispute is handled.
  • Understand that a payment can have legal effects. Even a small payment can, in some situations, restart the statute of limitations on a debt. Get advice if you are unsure.

Next steps

Debt validation is often an early step, not the only one. Depending on the collector's response, your next moves may include:

  • A 609 disclosure request — to get a fuller copy of your credit file from the bureaus
  • A follow-up after a “verified” response
  • Method of Verification letters — to ask how a bureau says it verified an item
  • Follow-up letters when a collector's validation looks incomplete

Each situation is different, and many take more than one round of letters.
