## Article 1: Why You Should Never Pay a Debt Collector Before You Dispute the Debt

A debt collector's demand is not proof.

The collector may have the wrong person, the wrong balance, incomplete records, an expired legal claim, or no adequate documentation showing that it has the authority to collect. Paying before examining those facts gives away your leverage—and may create additional problems.

Your default response should be: **Do not admit the debt. Do not promise payment. Dispute it and demand verification.**

### Why dispute first?

Debt is frequently bought and sold. By the time a collection company contacts you, the account may have passed through several businesses. Records can be missing, inaccurate, or attached to the wrong consumer.

Federal law requires a debt collector to provide validation information, including the creditor's name, the claimed amount, and information about your right to dispute the debt.

If you submit a written dispute within the applicable 30-day validation period, the collector generally must stop collecting the disputed debt until it sends verification. That is why you should respond promptly and in writing—not negotiate from a frightened telephone call.

### Before paying anything, determine:

- Is this actually your debt?
- Is the collector legitimate?
- Is the claimed balance accurate?
- Does the collector have authority to collect it?
- Is the debt too old to be enforced through a lawsuit?
- Could a payment or written acknowledgment restart the statute-of-limitations period under your state's law?
- What effect, if any, would payment have on your credit reports?

A payment does not necessarily remove a collection account from your credit history. With some old debts, even a small payment can potentially revive the collector's ability to sue, depending on state law.

### Disputing does not mean ignoring

Never ignore court papers. If a collector files a lawsuit and you fail to respond, it may obtain a default judgment without proving its case against an active defense. Dispute the claim, preserve your records, meet every court deadline, and consult a consumer-law attorney when necessary.

The principle is simple:

**A demand for money deserves investigation—not automatic obedience.**

Make the collector document its claim before you consider giving it anything.

## Short Post 1: Pressure Is Not Proof

A debt collector's urgency, confidence, and threats do not prove that you owe the amount demanded—or that the collector has the legal authority and documentation to collect it.

Don't surrender your leverage during a telephone call.

Don't admit the debt.  
Don't promise payment.  
Demand validation in writing.  
Preserve every communication.  
Dispute first.

If they have a legitimate, enforceable claim, make them establish it. Pressure is not proof.

## Draft: A Debt Collector Is Calling. Now What?

Debt-collection calls are designed to put you on the defensive. The caller asks the questions, creates urgency, and tries to get you talking before you have had a chance to think.

You do not have to play along.

Start here:

**“Who are you, what company do you represent, and what is the purpose of this call?”**

If they want you to verify your identity, address, or other personal information, your answer is simple:

**“I do not provide personal information to unsolicited callers. You called me. Give me your name, your company’s full legal name, its mailing address, and a telephone number.”**

Do not acknowledge the debt. Do not promise payment. Do not let a stranger who called you turn the conversation into an interrogation. Get the business information, end the call, write down what happened, and require the claim to be sent through the mail.
